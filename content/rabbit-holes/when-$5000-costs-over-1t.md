**$1,287,207,000,000**

What a fun number for kids who obsess over contemplating infinity and other ridiculously large whole numbers. Decillion is my favorite!

## Now, A Special Look at Why $5,000 Isn’t Really Worth $5,000

My kids **DO NOT** think $5,000 is a lot of money.

We live in a world where a six-figure income still means getting up and going to work every day. People are no longer dreaming about becoming millionaires. They are striving to put a capital **B** for Billionaire in front of their name.

My kids know better because they live in a household where we discuss the budget and the bills. They see money going into and out of their own bank accounts. They see food prices jump from one supply run to the next.

They see the cost of housing, electricity, insurance, Home Depot, transportation, phones, educational activities and everything else that gets paid before we even reach the things we *want* to do.

$5,000 might pay down a bill or two, but not pay them off. It may not solve an emergency. It may not even create breathing room when you have been waiting 13 months for a tax refund the IRS cannot explain why it has not been processed.

So, in the context of maintaining a family’s life, **$5,000 is not a lot of money.**

## The Unschooled Lesson: How the Number and the Value Are Not the Same Thing

On September 9, 2026, someone in power proposed giving adult U.S. citizens **$5,000** if one political party retained control of Congress. That someone apparently decided **$5,000 would sound much bigger than what $5,000 can buy.** That is precisely why I felt it necessary to discuss a country leader’s promise of $5,000 as such an interesting unschooling lesson.

That difference between a number and its value is something I want my kids to understand.

Now, we are **NOT** going to argue about politics.

We are going to do some math.

If approximately 240 million adults received **$5,000** each:

**240,000,000 × $5,000 = $1,200,000,000,000**

That is **$1.2 trillion**.

**You get $5,000.**

**The government needs to come up with roughly $1.2 trillion while already carrying more than $40 trillion in debt in order to give $5,000.**

Those statements describe the same proposal, but the second one gives my kids somewhere to go next: **Does the government already have that money? If so, where? If not, what gets taxed, cut, borrowed or created to produce it?**

Here is the first challenge:

**Never stop at the number someone wants you to look at.**

Ask what the number represents.

Then ask: **Does distributing $1.2 trillion cost exactly $1.2 trillion?** Answer No. Because …

## How Money Costs Money to Move

This is the part that disappears when we talk about government money as though someone changes a number on a screen and the job is finished.

Let’s pretend for a minute that the government has to print that $1.2 trillion in physical cash. (It wouldn’t. This is a thought experiment.) If the entire amount were printed as $20 bills because some merchants refuse to accept bills over $20, thus they would need: **60 billion individual $20 bills.**

The Federal Reserve’s published variable manufacturing cost for a $20 bill is about **7.3¢ per note**. That includes paper, ink, labor and direct manufacturing overhead. So, just to manufacture the $20 bills: **60 billion bills × $0.073 = $4.38 billion.**

Not $1.2 trillion. **$1.2 trillion plus another roughly $4.38 billion just to make the pieces of paper representing it.** And that still would not be the full cost.

The Federal Reserve spent about **$973 million in 2025 on the variable and fixed costs of producing new currency** during its normal currency program. Fixed costs include things such as facilities, people, research, manufacturing equipment. As we learn, the process to produce money carries a cost. And that still does not include every building, machine, security system, person, transportation network and process involved in creating and moving physical currency.

“Just print the money” is doing a lot of work in one little sentence.

## Now, What If They Mailed Everyone a Check?

Now we can run another thought experiment.

While the federal government is trying to phase out most paper checks and replace them with electronic payments, there is a problem. Not every adult in the U.S. has access to a bank account or electronic cash receptacle. But, suppose 240 million checks really had to go into 240 million envelopes.

At the current retail price of **82 cents for a one-ounce First-Class letter,** postage alone would have a retail-equivalent cost of: **240,000,000 × $0.82 = $196,800,000** Nearly **$197 million in postage.**

No, the government would not walk into the post office and buy 240 million Forever stamps. Bulk rates and government mailing arrangements are different. But they still need the check stock, envelopes, ink, printers, addresses databases, replacement checks, fraud prevention, and a team of customer support people to answer: **Where is my $5,000?**

The federal government has already said maintaining its broader paper-payment and paper-record infrastructure cost taxpayers more than **$657 million in one fiscal year**.

It means infrastructure is not free. Neither is an electronic infrastructure. Digital usually means cheaper, but it does not mean free.

## Now Where Would the $1.2 Trillion Come From?

This is where the lesson becomes considerably more interesting. The country already owes more than $40 trillion. So where does another $1.2 trillion come from?

Maybe something else gets cut? Like when we avoid the ice-cream aisle or stop going to the museum.

Maybe taxes pay for it. But then my kid can reasonably ask: **Doesn’t that mean taking money from me just to hand some of it back?**

If the money is borrowed, the cost does not stop at $1.2 trillion. Debt has interest.

Here’s another simple calculation I make sure my kids are aware of: while the government does not borrow at the prime rate, let’s use the current **6.75% prime rate** simply as an example.

**$1.2 trillion × 6.75% = $81 billion in one year**

That is **$81,000,000,000** for just one year.

This is the same consideration my kids need to know when looking at a house or car:

**The purchase price and the financing price are not the same thing.**

A $450,000 house does not cost a borrower only $450,000.

A $60,000 financed car does not ultimately cost only $60,000.

Borrowed money has a second price attached to it called interest!

## Where Did $1,287,207,000,000 Come From?

This is where $5,000 stops being a simple number.

Under this illustrative calculation, the first-year cost is not simply **$1.2 trillion**. It would be closer to:

**$1,287,207,000,000**

**One trillion, two hundred eighty-seven billion, two hundred seven million dollars.**

Here is our ridiculous stack thus far:

$1,200,000,000,000 - dividend payment

$4,380,000,000 - cost to print $20 bills

$973,000,000 - overhead cost to print the currency

$197,000,000 - for postage.

$657,000,000 - paper processing

$81,000,000,000 - interest for one year

**Total: $1,287,207,000,000. That is $87,207,000,000 more. 🤯**

Again, this is **not an estimate of the actual program cost.** They would not print the entire amount in cash *and* mail everyone a check. We stacked the numbers because that is what this unschooling exercise is about. But…

**What else haven’t we counted?**

Just like that, a $5,000 promise sounds significantly more complicated than a Christmas gift from your granddad the year he knows will be his last and your step-grandmother has already written you out of the will.

## Now What Would $5,000 Actually Do for Us?

Because the number printed on money does not represent fixed purchasing power. $40 being enough to purchase groceries for the week is no longer the case. This is probably the most important part for my kids. A $20 bill always says $20. But $20 does not always buy the same amount of life.

What is the cost of mortgage or rent, groceries, electricity & Internet, home & car insurance, gas and household supplies?

Now add them. Does $5,000 cover one month? For some families, depending on where they live, maybe. For others, no.

**And we have not even talked about fixing damage our home insurance, which we pay for, denied coverage on.**

Or retirement savings, an emergency fund, replacing engineered for obsolescence appliances, martial arts, art supplies or the dozens of other things human work to be able to enjoy the experience of.

That distinction matters. Because **money is not valuable because the number is large. Money is valuable because of what the number allows us to obtain.**

My kids already understand this. Seaweed, a staple in one of my kid’s diets, jumped from **$2.92 for a six-pack to $4.99.** No gradual climb. Just $2.92 for months. Then $4.99. The seaweed still comes home because it is a staple.

But other things do not make it to our shopping cart. No ice cream. No mangoes. No Lemonade. I will be making our nut mylk from scratch and we still have to sacrifice with one less day of martial arts practice each week.

**That is what reduced purchasing power looks like in our house.**

The dollars are still there. They just buy less life.

## Now, the Illusion of Large Figures

That **$5,000** pandering is also why I don’t want my kids impressed by the phrase six-figure salary. Someone can make $100,000 a year and technically have a six-figure income. That used to sound like comfort & security. But not after ever increasing inflation and an income-tax refund that never arrived.

My generation is learning faster than ever that a household can earn more dollars than it did several years ago, like 2019, and still have less purchasing power in 2026. Yep your income can go up while your standard of living goes down.

So I don’t want my kids dazzled by a **$5,000** one-time payment any more than I want them dazzled by a six-figure salary that does not explain which end of the ½ million dollar scale that six-figures is on. Both are numbers without context.

**What can it buy?** That’s the question.

$5000: I mean come on, look how many zeros the thousands place is missing!

## Now Let’s Talk About the Word “Dividend”

The proposed **$5,000** payment was called a **dividend**.

Interesting choice of word. A dividend normally suggests something produced a return and you are receiving your share.

Now is a good time to challenge our kids not just about the value of money but the value of words. They can define the words first.

**Vocabulary:**

Dividend. Relief. Benefit. Refund. Stimulus. Incentive. Investment. Savings. Free. Reward. Cut. Reform. Bribe. Lobbying.

## What Do Choice Words Encourage Us to Notice?

Then we can discuss what makes something a bribe versus lobbying, an incentive, a campaign promise or pandering? Not only was that a statement made during a political campaign event, it raises so many more questions about intent and the word bribe and incentive come into play.

Like: Is directly offering money in exchange for someone’s specific vote different from promising a financial benefit if voters give one political party power?

I’m not answering those questions for my kids. Because we’re no longer talking about whether **$5,000** sounds nice, we’re now investigating a financial claim and what that means.

The proposal was not: Show me that you voted this way and I will hand you **$5,000.** It was if an outcome happens you will be paid.

**But it’s 2026 Legally, What are words anymore???**

That does not mean the questioning has to stop.

Why announce a payment before an election?

Why connect it to an electoral outcome?

What response is the promise designed to produce?

When does an incentive become pandering?

When does pandering become manipulation?

When does manipulation cross a legal line?

Would we hear the same proposal differently if it came after the election?

These are questions. I don’t need to tell my children which answers they are supposed to reach. They just need these real-world concerns put in front of them **now**, while they have nothing but time to learn how to interrogate them.

Before they sell their reproductive rights for **$5,000**.

Oh wait. We already did that.

Damn. Where’s my check?

**What happens to the purchasing power of that $5,000 after we receive it?**

That is a very different conversation from:

**Yay! Five thousand dollars!**

Is that the part we care about as parents unschooling with our kids?

And that’s another lesson the world handed my unschooled kids without me having to manufacture a curriculum.
