> This Lab Note continues the rabbit hole opened in [Part 3: Are We Even Present for Our Kids?](https://unschoolpages.substack.com/p/are-we-even-present-for-our-kids)

A childhood can fill with “laters” that never arrive, or it can contain boundaries surrounded by dependable returns.

I have seen later disappear into abandonment.

I have seen later change into never.

I have seen later become disgust after a kid asks again and again because no date, time, explanation, or guidance was ever offered for them to hold on to.

“Later” becomes the vague distance where kids’ invitations are sent to disappear.

## When Gentle Requests Stop Working

At first, a kid may ask gently.

Can you watch this?

Can I tell you something?

Can we spend time together?

Can you help me?

When nothing comes back, they ask again. Then louder. Then with frustration. Then with all the pent-up emotion of someone who has been repeatedly told to wait without ever being given anything real to wait for.

Eventually, the adult responds to the accumulated hurt rather than the original request.

“I just can’t with you at all!”

Suddenly, the kid’s so-called attitude becomes the problem.

The outburst becomes the problem.

The repeated asking becomes the problem.

The kid is blamed for the visible reaction to needs that have been routinely neglected.

How often do we accuse kids of demanding attention after repeatedly teaching them that gentle requests will not reach us?

A lack of presence does not always break a relationship dramatically. Sometimes it wears the relationship down through ordinary repetition.

One forgotten promise.

One interrupted story.

One more “not right now.”

One more sigh when the kid enters the room.

One more moment in which their excitement is met with irritation.

One more day in which the adults they love appear to have time for work, phones, television, chores, friends, obligations, and everyone else—but not for them.

## When Silence Looks Like Maturity

Eventually, kids may stop asking.

Adults can mistake that silence for maturity, independence, or improved behavior. But sometimes the kid has simply accepted that the relationship is not a dependable place to bring their inner life.

They begin to keep their stories elsewhere.

Their questions go elsewhere.

Their fears, ideas, mistakes, discoveries, and celebrations go elsewhere.

The child who once followed us from room to room may become the teenager who stays behind a closed door.

Then adults wonder why they do not communicate.

We ask why they stay in their rooms.

We complain that they do not tell us anything.

We become suspicious of the people who now know them better than we do.

But relationships do not suddenly begin at adolescence. The teenage relationship is built through thousands of ordinary childhood invitations—and through what happened when those invitations reached us.

Did we turn toward them?

Did we ask for a moment and then return?

Did we remember what they were waiting to share?

Did they have to create a crisis before we treated their need as real?

## The Relationship Is Not Included With Food and Shelter

Kids are often treated as though the relationship does not require the same care we expect in every other meaningful connection.

We would not repeatedly ignore a friend and then blame them for becoming distant.

We would not postpone every conversation with a partner and expect intimacy to remain untouched.

We would not tell another adult that their needs, opinions, and disappointments do not matter because we bought the groceries or paid the mortgage.

Yet kids are expected to absorb neglect without allowing it to change how they feel about us.

They are expected to remain affectionate, open, compliant, and grateful while receiving very little control over the terms of the relationship.

Providing food, shelter, clothing, and transportation does not automatically create closeness.

Those things matter. They are also responsibilities adults accept when we bring kids into our lives.

Relationship is built through what happens around those responsibilities: how we listen, how we respond, how we repair, how we explain our limits, and whether our kids can trust us to come back.

## Then Comes the Eighteen-Year Release

For some adults, childhood is treated like a sentence everyone is serving together.

The parent endures the kid until eighteen.

The kid endures the home until they are legally free to leave.

We joke about putting them out.

We count down the years.

We say, “When you turn eighteen, you’re on your own.”

Sometimes the joke is delivered as motivation. Sometimes it is a threat. Sometimes it is simply the family’s accepted plan.

Either way, eighteen becomes the expiration date on belonging.

The message is clear: you may live here while the law requires me to keep you. After that, your place in this home is negotiable.

What does it teach a kid when belonging can be revoked whenever the relationship becomes difficult?

What kind of attachment are we building when the underlying message is that home is temporary, patience is conditional, and adulthood is the date of release?

Why is relationship-building treated as essential between adults but optional with kids?

Why do we expect kids to respect us without consistently experiencing our respect?

Why do we demand honesty from them while making honesty unsafe?

Why do we want them to come to us with major problems after years of showing them that their smaller needs were already too much?

## Send Them Somewhere Else

The eighteen-year release does not always wait until eighteen.

When a kid’s needs, pain, behavior, resistance, or difference becomes inconvenient, adults may begin looking for somewhere else to send them.

Send them to the other parent.

Send them to a relative.

Send them to boarding school.

Send them to a program.

Send them somewhere another adult can deal with the parts of them we no longer want to face.

There may be circumstances in which a different living arrangement is necessary for safety or stability. But using removal as a threat teaches something very different.

It teaches that belonging is conditional.

It teaches that home is safe only while the kid remains manageable.

It teaches that conflict may cost them their family.

A kid should not have to wonder whether one bad day, one unmet expectation, one argument, one difference of opinion, or one overwhelming season will make the adults in their life send them away.

The breakdown does not begin when the kid finally leaves.

It begins each time they approach and learn that reaching for us carries rejection, disgust, punishment, or the threat of removal.

## Some Kids Are Not Leaving to Begin Their Lives

By eighteen, some kids are not leaving home to begin their lives.

They are escaping the life they were forced to survive.

They are not moving toward independence with the safety of a dependable home behind them. They are running from unlivable conditions, counting down until no one can legally make them return.

The countdown to eighteen may look like motivation from the outside. Inside the child, it may be a survival clock.

Just a few more years.

Just a few more months.

Just a few more days until I can get out.

And when a kid has been starved for welcome, attention, guidance, protection, or affection, that hunger does not disappear because they become legally adult.

It may make them vulnerable to anyone willing to imitate the care they were denied.

Someone who listens can feel like safety.

Someone who offers a place to stay can feel like family.

Someone who says, “I understand you better than they do,” can gain enormous influence over a young person who has spent years desperate to be known.

This is not because the young person is foolish.

It is because relational hunger makes borrowed belonging feel like rescue.

## Stop Counting to Eighteen

Ending the eighteen-year release does not mean preventing our kids from growing up.

It does not mean keeping them dependent.

It does not mean removing boundaries, consequences, financial realities, or expectations from family life.

It means removing eighteen as the end of belonging.

Our kids should be able to leave because the world is calling them—not because remaining with us has become unbearable.

They should be able to explore, work, travel, build relationships, make mistakes, change direction, and create lives of their own while knowing there is still a relationship safe enough to return to.

Home does not have to remain their permanent address to remain part of their foundation.

Adulthood does not erase the need for family. It changes the relationship.

The adult child becomes responsible for their choices, labor, money, boundaries, repair, and participation. The parent is no longer managing a child’s life. But neither person has to pretend that love and belonging expired when legal responsibility did.

Eighteen can be a threshold.

It does not have to be a release date.

## Later Needs Somewhere to Land

That is why “later” matters long before eighteen arrives.

Later is not harmless simply because it postpones instead of openly refusing.

Later needs somewhere to land.

It needs a time, a conversation, a reminder, a return, or an honest renegotiation.

Sometimes later means the original plan happens exactly as promised.

Sometimes money changes.

Sometimes responsibilities were not completed.

Sometimes the answer becomes no.

Sometimes everyone has to sit with disappointment.

Dependable return is not the promise that kids will always receive what they want. It is the promise that their request will not be discarded simply because addressing it is inconvenient.

A kid should not have to become disruptive enough to force us to remember that they were waiting.

In my home, later always comes.

Not always as a perfect one-to-one fulfillment. Not always on the original day. Not always with the answer my kids hoped to receive.

But we return to the conversation.

We remember what mattered.

We explain what changed.

We negotiate when negotiation is possible.

We acknowledge disappointment.

We do not use patience as an opportunity to forget.

That ordinary practice is connected to the larger life I want to build with my kids.

I do not want them counting down to the day they can escape me.

I want them growing toward a life that belongs to them while knowing our relationship still belongs to all of us.

The alternative to the eighteen-year release is not holding on tighter.

It is building a relationship our kids do not have to flee in order to become free.
