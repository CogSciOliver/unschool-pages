# What Is Deschooling?

**Deschooling is the ongoing work of deconditioning from authoritarian childhood, parenting, educational, cultural, and relational patterns so they are not unconsciously reproduced within self-directed family relationships.**

That is the definition I use throughout Unschool Pages and The Deschooling Lab.

Deschooling is not simply unlearning school.

It is not only taking a child out of school and waiting for them to decompress. It is not just dropping grades, curriculum, schedules, compulsory reading, testing, or cursive handwriting. Those can all become part of deschooling, but they are not the whole of it.

The deeper work is in the adult.

Deschooling asks us to examine what childhood taught us about authority, obedience, performance, respect, control, success, fear, punishment, adulthood, parenting, and what children are supposedly allowed to know or decide for themselves.

School is one place those ideas may have been reinforced. It is not the only place they came from.

They may also come from family systems, religion, culture, economic pressure, gender expectations, social class, racism, respectability politics, professional norms, or the simple repetition of "this is how things are done."

## Deschooling is deconditioning from authoritarian childhood

Many adults were raised inside systems where authority flowed in one direction.

Adults knew.

Children obeyed.

Adults decided what was true, what was appropriate, what was important, what counted as success, and when a child had earned more freedom.

A child could be expected to comply before they understood, agree before they were convinced, perform gratitude before they felt it, offer respect without receiving it, or suppress their own perception because an adult's interpretation carried more authority.

That conditioning does not disappear because someone decides to unschool.

A parent can remove school from a child's life and still recreate school inside the home.

They can still panic when reading does not happen on schedule.

They can still demand visible proof that learning is happening.

They can still measure a child's worth through productivity.

They can still force handwriting because "you need to know this."

They can still treat technology as something a child must earn.

They can still confuse obedience with respect.

They can still believe autonomy is theirs to grant and withdraw.

Deschooling is the work of noticing those impulses before they become the architecture of the relationship.

## Deschooling is not permissiveness

Rejecting authoritarian control does not require pretending children need no help, protection, limits, resources, knowledge, or adult participation.

Children live in families. Families have needs. Bodies have limits. Money is finite. Other people have boundaries. Roads, water, fire, machines, illness, laws, and physical reality do not disappear because a family values autonomy.

The question is not whether adults participate.

The question is **how**.

Are we sharing information or demanding submission?

Are we protecting a child from a real danger or protecting ourselves from discomfort?

Are we helping build competence or maintaining dependence?

Are we setting a boundary around our own body, time, money, or capacity, or are we controlling another person's body, time, attention, and interests because control feels more familiar than negotiation?

Deschooling does not remove relationship. It asks us to become more conscious of the power we bring into it.

## Deschooling is shadow work

Some of the most difficult conditioning stays invisible until a child's autonomy touches it.

You may believe in self-directed learning until your child does not want to read at the age you expected.

You may believe in bodily autonomy until your child refuses affection from a relative.

You may believe in curiosity until their curiosity lives almost entirely inside a computer.

You may believe children deserve respect until they disagree with you in public.

You may believe grades do not matter until someone asks what level your child is on.

You may believe you have rejected conventional parenting until your child behaves in a way that makes you feel judged as a parent.

Then the old script shows up.

Make them do it.

Make them stop.

Make them look respectful.

Make them prove they are learning.

Make them perform childhood correctly so I can perform parenting correctly.

That moment is part of deschooling.

The child did not create the pattern. Their autonomy exposed it.

The work is ours.

## Deschooling includes reparenting ourselves

For many adults, authoritarian conditioning was not theoretical.

It was embodied.

It may have involved school trauma, parental trauma, religious trauma, humiliation, punishment, forced performance, fear of failure, fear of adults, fear of disappointing authority, or learning that safety depended on being easy to control.

Those experiences can continue shaping adult behavior long after we intellectually reject them.

This is why deschooling often includes reparenting.

We may have to give ourselves permission we never received.

Permission to rest without earning it.

Permission to be a beginner.

Permission to change our minds.

Permission to learn something without monetizing it.

Permission to say no to authority.

Permission to recognize that an adult can be wrong.

Permission to stop performing competence and admit we do not know.

Permission to build a family relationship that does not resemble the one we inherited.

Reparenting does not mean pretending the past did not happen. It means becoming conscious of what the past trained us to reproduce.

## Performative parenting is one of the patterns deschooling asks us to confront

A great deal of traditional parenting is organized around appearance.

Does the child look respectful?

Do they appear well behaved?

Are they reading on time?

Are they progressing at the expected pace?

Do they make the family look successful?

Does the parent appear to be in control?

That is different from asking whether respect, competence, trust, curiosity, emotional safety, or understanding actually exist.

I call this **performative functioning**.

Performative functioning happens when the visible signs of a functioning family become more important than what is actually happening inside the relationships.

A child can say "yes, ma'am" and feel no respect.

A child can receive an A and understand very little.

A family can appear close while nobody feels safe telling the truth.

A child can perform gratitude while carrying resentment.

A parent can appear completely in control while the relationship underneath that control is deteriorating.

Deschooling asks us to stop confusing the performance with the thing itself.

## Deschooling and unschooling are related, but they are not the same thing

[Unschooling](/unschooling/) concerns the conditions in which children can direct increasingly significant portions of their own learning, interests, development, and lives.

Deschooling concerns the work adults do within themselves so inherited authoritarian patterns do not poison those conditions.

Unschooling can remove external systems of control.

Deschooling addresses the control systems that remain inside us.

That is why deschooling is not something a family completes before they become "real unschoolers."

It is ongoing.

A new age, new technology, new relationship, new risk, new interest, new conflict, or new expression of autonomy can expose conditioning that had no reason to surface before.

A four-year-old can expose one layer.

A twelve-year-old can expose another.

A teenager with global technological access can expose beliefs about knowledge, authority, safety, privacy, work, relationships, and competence that their parent had never previously been required to examine.

The work continues because the relationship continues changing.

## The questions deschooling asks

Deschooling is not a checklist of correct parenting behaviors. It is an ongoing inquiry into what is driving us when we feel the urge to control.

I return to questions like these:

**Why do I believe this must happen?**

**Whose expectation am I reproducing?**

**What am I afraid will happen if I do not control this?**

**Am I responding to an actual danger, or to a learned fear of noncompliance?**

**Do I want competence and understanding, or do I want visible proof that makes me feel like I am doing parenting correctly?**

**Am I protecting my child, or protecting an inherited idea about what childhood should look like?**

**Am I responding to the child in front of me, or to authority structures that still live inside me?**

Those questions are not always comfortable.

That is the point of The Deschooling Lab.

The lab is where I examine the contradictions, old scripts, emotional reactions, inherited expectations, and childhood wounds that can follow us into a family even after we have consciously chosen something different.

## A working definition for Unschool Pages

When I use the word **deschooling** here, I mean more than leaving school behind.

I mean the ongoing work of deconditioning authority, control, coercion, performance, and inherited expectations within ourselves so that we do not unconsciously rebuild those systems around our children.

It includes letting go of school-based expectations when they interfere with self-directed learning.

It includes examining parental, religious, cultural, and institutional conditioning.

It includes shadow work.

It includes reparenting.

It includes learning to distinguish guidance from control, protection from restriction, relationship from performance, and respect from compliance.

Most of all, it means accepting that removing an authoritarian system from a child's environment does not automatically remove that system from the adults who were shaped by it.

That part is our work.
